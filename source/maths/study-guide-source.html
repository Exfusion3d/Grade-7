Expressions, Formulae & Equations

Turning words into algebra, working out values, tidying expressions, expanding brackets, solving equations and inequalities.

Big idea: A letter stands for a number we don't know yet. Algebra has rules for writing, tidying and solving with letters, and every rule works the same way it does with ordinary numbers. If you're stuck, try a real number first.

2.1 Writing expressions

An expression is a maths recipe with a letter in it, like y + 3: "whatever y is, add 3". There is no = sign in an expression.

The method

  1. Find the letter (the unknown number).
  2. Turn each word into a symbol: more than / plus / increased by → +; less than / take away / decreased by → −; times / multiplied by / twice → ×; divided by / half → ÷.
  3. Do the steps in the order the question says.
  4. Write it the short algebra way: no × sign, number first (3 × y = 3y, p × q = pq), and ÷ as a fraction (y ÷ 2 = y2).
A number machine: y goes in, is multiplied by 4, then 1 is added. Out comes 4y + 1.

2.2 Substituting into expressions and formulae

Substitute means "swap in". When you know what the letter is worth, put that number in place of the letter, then work it out. A formula is a rule with an = sign that links letters, like M = P − E.

The method

  1. Write the expression (or formula) down first.
  2. Rewrite it with each letter swapped for its number. Put back the hidden signs: 2m means 2 × m, ab means a × b, and a fraction line means ÷.
  3. Work it out: × and ÷ before + and −. Work out the top of a fraction first.
  4. For a formula, if the letter you want is not on its own, rearrange first (M = P − E gives P = M + E).
Swap each letter for its number, put the × back in, then work it out: 14 + 6 = 20.

2.3 Simplifying and like terms

Like terms have exactly the same letter part: 5x and 2x are like terms; 5x and 2y are not. To simplify, add or take away the numbers in front of like terms. Think of x as "apples": 5 apples + 4 apples = 9 apples.

The method

  1. Find the like terms. Each term keeps the sign in front of it (− 3p goes with the minus).
  2. Move the like terms next to each other, signs and all.
  3. Add or take away the numbers in front. A letter on its own counts as 1 (x = 1x), and you write 1x as just x.
  4. Numbers with no letter are like terms too: collect them on their own.
An algebra pyramid: each block is the two blocks under it added. 3x + 5x = 8x, 5x + x = 6x, 8x + 6x = 14x.

2.4 Expanding brackets

Expand (or "multiply out") means get rid of the brackets. The number outside multiplies every term inside: 3(y + 6) = 3 × y + 3 × 6 = 3y + 18.

The method

  1. Multiply the number outside by the first term inside.
  2. Multiply it by the next term, and keep the sign in front of that term (+ stays +, − stays −).
  3. Do the same for every term inside: there may be three.
  4. Check: one term out for each term in.
Area = 8(3y + 4). Split the rectangle in two: 8 × 3y = 24y and 8 × 4 = 32, so the area is 24y + 32.

2.5 Solving equations

An equation has an = sign: both sides are worth the same, like a balanced scale. Solving means finding the number the letter stands for. Whatever you do to one side, do to the other side, and it stays balanced.

The method

  1. Look at what is being done to x.
  2. Do the opposite to both sides: + ↔ −, × ↔ ÷. (x + 6: take away 6. x − 6: add 6. 2x: divide by 2.)
  3. Keep going until x is on its own: x = …
  4. Check: put your answer back into the first equation. Both sides must match.
x + 6 = 10 on a balance. Take 6 from both pans and it still balances: x = 4.

2.6 Inequalities

An inequality says one side is bigger than the other. < means "is less than" and > means "is greater than". x > 4 means x could be any number bigger than 4, but not 4 itself.

The method

  1. Read the sign from left to right: x < 10 is "x is less than 10"; x > 10 is "x is greater than 10".
  2. The open (wide) end of the sign faces the bigger number.
  3. On a number line: an open circle on the number (it is not allowed), and an arrow towards the numbers that work.
  4. An integer is a whole number (it can be negative). For y > 4, the smallest integer is the next whole number up: 5.
y > 4: an open circle at 4 (4 is not allowed) and an arrow to the right. The integers that work are 5, 6, 7, …

Decimals

Ordering and comparing decimals, quick mental methods, multiplying decimals, dividing decimals and sharing money.

Big idea: Every digit in a decimal has a place value: ones, then tenths, hundredths, thousandths. Line the digits up by place value and decimals work just like whole numbers. Always estimate first, so you can spot a decimal point in the wrong place.

4.1 Ordering and comparing decimals

To compare two decimals, compare them place by place, starting from the left. The first place where the digits differ decides which number is bigger.

The method

  1. Compare the whole-number parts first. If one is bigger, that number is bigger.
  2. If they are the same, line up the decimal points and pad with zeros so both have the same number of decimal places (2.19 → 2.190).
  3. Compare the tenths, then the hundredths, then the thousandths, until two digits are different.
  4. Write the sign: < means "is smaller than", > means "is bigger than". = means equal, ≠ means "not equal".
Line up the place values and pad 2.19 to 2.190. The ones are equal, so look at the tenths: 1 tenth is less than 2 tenths.

4.2 Mental methods with decimals

Many decimal sums can be done in your head. Split numbers into wholes and decimals, or round one number to a whole number and then adjust. For harder ones, use a written column method.

The method

  1. Split: add (or take away) the wholes, then the decimals, then put them together. 12.7 + 4.5 → 16 + 1.2 = 17.2.
  2. Zara's method for subtracting: wholes first, then decimals. The decimal part may go below zero: 7.4 − 2.6 → 5 and −0.2 → 4.8.
  3. Round and adjust: round one number to a whole, work it out, then undo the rounding. Added too much? Take it back off. Took away too much? Add it back.
  4. Times by a decimal: 0.3 is 3 tenths, so 4 × 0.3 = 12 tenths = 1.2.
  5. Written method: line up the decimal points, pad with zeros, then add or subtract in columns.
4.3 + 7.9: add 8 instead (easy!) to get 12.3. That was 0.1 too much, so take 0.1 back off: 12.2.

4.4 Dividing decimals and money problems

To divide a decimal by a whole number, use short division (the "bus stop"). The decimal point in the answer goes straight above the point in the number. Sharing money equally is just division.

The method

  1. Estimate first: 18.25 ÷ 5 ≈ 20 ÷ 5 = 4.
  2. Write the bus stop. Put the decimal point in the answer straight above the one inside.
  3. Divide digit by digit from the left. Carry each remainder to the next digit.
  4. If the divisor has two digits (12, 15, 25), use the first two or three digits together: 27 ÷ 12 = 2 r 3.
  5. Check by multiplying back: 3.65 × 5 = 18.25 ✔. For money, write the answer with 2 decimal places and a $ sign.

Big idea: Data is information we collect to answer a question. First decide what kind of data it is. Then use one number, an average, to describe the whole set: the mode, the median or the mean.

6.1 Types of data and questionnaires

Every piece of data is one of three types. Ask yourself: is it a word, a number you count, or a number you measure?

The method

  3. Is it a number you measure that can be any value, even 1.37 (mass, height, time, length)? It is continuous.

6.2 Mode, median and mean

An average is one number that sums up a whole set of data. There are three kinds, and each is found in its own way.

The method

  1. Mode: the value that appears most often. Count how many times each value appears.
  2. Median: the middle value. First put the numbers in order, smallest to largest. With an odd number of values, take the one in the middle. With an even number of values there are two in the middle: add them and divide by 2.
  3. Mean: add up all the values, then divide by how many values there are.

Sequences & Functions

Doing calculations in the right order, working with negative numbers, finding rules for sequences and patterns, the nth term, and function machines.

Big idea: Maths follows rules. BIDMAS tells you the order to work in. A sequence has a rule that makes each term. A function machine has a rule that turns each input into an output. Find the rule, and you can work out anything.

9.1 Order of operations & negative numbers

When a calculation has more than one operation, everyone must do them in the same order, or we would all get different answers. That order is called BIDMAS.

The method

  1. Brackets first: work out anything inside ( ).
  2. Indices next: powers like 3² = 3 × 3 = 9.
  3. Divide and Multiply next. They are equal, so do them from left to right.
  4. Add and Subtract last. They are also equal, so do them from left to right.
  5. A fraction line works like a bracket: 12 + 93 means (12 + 9) ÷ 3.
  6. Negatives: adding moves right on the number line, subtracting moves left. For × and ÷: same signs give +, different signs give −.
The BIDMAS ladder: start at the top. ÷ and × share a step, and so do + and −.
−2 + 6: start at −2 and jump 6 to the right. You land on 4.

9.2 Sequences & patterns

A sequence is a list of numbers that follow a rule. Each number is a term. The term-to-term rule tells you how to get from one term to the next.

The method

  1. Look at the gap between each term and the next one.
  2. If the gap is the same each time, the rule is add (going up) or subtract (going down) that number. Some rules multiply or divide instead.
  3. Use the rule on the last term to get the next terms.
  4. For missing terms in the middle, find the rule from two terms that are next to each other, then fill the gaps.
  5. A sequence that ends is finite. A sequence with "…" at the end goes on for ever: it is infinite.
7, 11, 15, 19 … The gap is always 4, so the term-to-term rule is "add 4". The next two terms are 23 and 27.

9.3 The nth term & function machines

The nth term rule links each term to its position number n (1st, 2nd, 3rd …). With it you can jump straight to any term, like the 20th, without listing them all. A function machine uses a rule to turn an input into an output.

The method

  1. Make a table: position number 1, 2, 3 … on top, term underneath.
  2. If the terms are the times table of a number (6, 12, 18 …), the rule is "term = 6 × position", so the nth term is 6n.
  3. If the terms go up by 1 (10, 11, 12 …), compare each term with its position: 10 is 9 more than 1, so the nth term is n + 9.
  4. To find a term, put its position in for n: the 10th term of 6n is 6 × 10 = 60.
  5. Function machine: do the rule to each input to get the output. To go backwards from an output, use the inverse (opposite): + ↔ −, × ↔ ÷.

Percentages

Changing between percentages, fractions and decimals, and finding fractions and percentages of amounts.

Big idea: Per cent means "out of 100". 64% is 64 out of every 100, which is 64100 or 0.64. A percentage, a fraction and a decimal are three ways of writing the same amount.

10.1 Percentages, fractions and decimals

To change between the three forms, you only need two moves: ÷ 100 to leave a percentage, and × 100 to make one.

The method

  1. Percentage → fraction: write the number over 100, then simplify: divide the top and bottom by the same number until you can't any more. If the percentage has a decimal point (7.5%), multiply the top and bottom by 10 first: 7.5100 = 751000.
  2. Percentage → decimal: divide by 100. The digits move two places to the right of the point: 40% = 0.40 = 0.4, 4% = 0.04.
  3. Fraction → percentage: multiply by 100%. Turn the fraction into a decimal first (top ÷ bottom), then × 100: 14 = 0.25, and 0.25 × 100 = 25%.
  4. Mixed number → percentage: the whole number 1 is 100%. So 112 = 1.5 = 150%. Percentages over 100% mean "more than the whole".

10.2 Percentages of amounts

"Of" means multiply. To find a fraction or a percentage of an amount, cut the amount into equal parts, then take the parts you need.

The method

  1. Fraction of an amount: divide by the bottom, then multiply by the top. 34 of 60 g: 60 ÷ 4 = 15, then 15 × 3 = 45 g.
  2. 10% of an amount: divide by 10. 1%: divide by 100 (or divide the 10% by 10).
  3. 0.5% is half of 1%, and 0.1% is 1% ÷ 10.
  4. Build the percentage you want by adding chunks: 3.5% = 1% + 1% + 1% + 0.5%.
600 kg is 100%. Cut it into ten 10% chunks of 60 kg, then cut one chunk into ten 1% pieces of 6 kg.

🤖 Maths Cheat Sheet

Words → algebra

  • more than, plus, increased by → +
  • less than, take away, decreased by → −
  • times, multiplied by, twice → ×
  • divided by, shared, half → ÷
  • 3 × y = 3y · p × q = pq · y ÷ 2 = y2
  • x less than y = y − x (start with y)
  • subtract from 52 → 52 − …

Substitute & simplify

  • Swap each letter for its number, then use BIDMAS: 2m + n, m = 7, n = 6 → 14 + 6 = 20
  • ab means a × b; 2h means 2 × h
  • Like terms have the same letter: 5x + 4x = 9x · x on its own = 1x
  • Collect each kind: 2g + 8 + 3g − 5 = 5g + 3
  • Pyramid: each block = the two below added

Brackets & equations

  • Expand: multiply every term inside: 2(3b − 4) = 6b − 8
  • Solve: do the inverse to both sides: + ↔ −, × ↔ ÷
  • x − 18 = 30 → x = 30 + 18 = 48 · 12x = 72 → x = 6
  • Check by putting the answer back in

Inequalities

  • < less than · > greater than: the small end points at the smaller number
  • x < 3: open circle at 3, arrow left
  • y > −7: smallest integer is −6

Decimals: order & mental

  • Line up the points; fill gaps with zeros: 2.19 = 2.190 < 2.205
  • Compare digit by digit from the left
  • Round, then adjust: 4.3 + 7.9 = 4.3 + 8 − 0.1 = 12.2
  • 3 × 0.004 = 0.012 (3 × 4 = 12, three decimal places)

× and ÷ decimals

  • ×: ignore the points, multiply, count the decimal places, put them back: 3.2 × 52 → 32 × 52 = 1664 → 166.4
  • ÷: bus stop, point goes straight up: 18.25 ÷ 5 = 3.65
  • Estimate first, check by multiplying back
  • Money: 2 decimal places ($3.60)

Data

  • Count → discrete · Measure → continuous · Words → categorical
  • Mode = most common
  • Median = middle, after sorting (two middles: halfway)
  • Mean = total ÷ how many: 290 ÷ 20 = 14.5

BIDMAS & negatives

  • Brackets → Indices → D M (equal, left to right) → A S (equal, left to right)
  • A fraction line means ÷: 123 + 9 = 4 + 9 = 13
  • −2 + 6 = 4 (move right) · 9 − 12 = −3
  • × ÷: same signs → +, different signs → −: −2 × 3 = −6

Sequences & machines

  • Term-to-term rule: what you do to get the next term (add 4)
  • nth term: position → term. Jumps of 5 starting at 5 → 5n (not n + 5)
  • 5n: 8th term = 40, 20th = 100
  • Jumps of 1: n + 21 → 22, 23, 24…
  • Inverse machine: undo each step, in reverse order

Percentages

  • Per cent = out of 100
  • % → decimal: ÷ 100 · decimal → %: × 100
  • % → fraction: over 100, then simplify: 64% = 64100 = 1625
  • 7.5% = 0.075 = 340
  • 1 12 = 150% (more than one whole)

% of an amount

  • 10% = ÷ 10 · 1% = ÷ 100 · 50% = half · 25% = quarter
  • Build it: 2.2% of 600 kg = 2 × 6 + 0.2 × 6 = 13.2 kg
  • 34 of 60 g: ÷ 4, × 3 = 45 g

Stuck? 🤖

  • Try a real number first, then do the same with the letter
  • Estimate before you calculate
  • Check: put your answer back in, or work backwards
