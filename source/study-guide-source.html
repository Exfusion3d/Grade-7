4

Grouping & Identifying Organisms

Living or not living? Same species or not? And how do scientists sort millions of living things?

Big idea: All living things do the same seven life processes. Scientists use these to decide what is alive, and they sort living things into groups (like species) so the huge variety of life is easier to study.

4.1 The seven life processes (MRS GREN)

To count as alive, an organism must be able to do all seven of these things. An organism is just a fancy word for "a living thing".

Memory trickMeet MRS GREN. Each letter is one life process: Movement, Respiration, Sensitivity, Growth, Reproduction, Excretion, Nutrition.
Life processWhat it meansExample
MMovementChanging the shape or position of part or all of the body.A cheetah running; a sunflower turning to face the Sun.
RRespirationBreaking down nutrients (food) inside cells to release energy.Your cells break down sugar from breakfast to give you energy for football.
SSensitivityDetecting changes in the environment and responding to them.A polar bear smells meat from far away with its nose.
GGrowthA permanent increase in size and mass. Young plants and animals get bigger.A baby zebra growing into an adult zebra.
RReproductionMaking new individuals of the same kind of organism ("producing young ones").A zebra having a foal; a plant making seeds.
EExcretionGetting rid of waste materials the body has made.Animals excrete carbon dioxide when they breathe out.
NNutritionFeeding: taking in materials for energy and growth. Another word for nutrition is "feeding".A lion eating meat; a plant making its own food.
Watch out!Respiration is NOT the same as breathing. Breathing just moves air in and out. Respiration is the chemical process in every cell that releases energy from food. A plant respires but has no lungs!
Watch out!"Growth" must be permanent. A balloon getting bigger, or you getting puffy after a big lunch, is not growth. Growth is a lasting increase in size and mass.

4.2 Plants vs animals

How plants get food

Plants make their own food by photosynthesis. They need three things:

  • ☀️ Sunlight (energy)
  • 💧 Water (from the soil, through the roots)
  • 💨 Carbon dioxide (from the air, through the leaves)

So an oak tree can make its own food, but a mushroom, an earthworm and a lion cannot.

Watch out!A mushroom is NOT a plant. It is a fungus and cannot use sunlight to make food.

Why animals move but plants stay put

Two reasons, which is why the answer is "Both A and C":

  • A: Plants do not have muscles or legs to walk.
  • C: Animals must go and find their food. Plants make their food right where they are.
Real lifeA plant is like a kitchen that cooks its own meals. An animal has to go shopping!

4.3 Viruses: alive or not?

Viruses make us ill (a virus can even make a zebra ill!), but most scientists say viruses are NOT living.

  • Viruses are very small, much smaller than a cell.
  • Viruses are not made of cells. They are just a coat of protein with instructions (genetic material) inside.
  • Viruses can only replicate (make copies of themselves) when they are inside a living cell.
  • They cannot carry out the seven life processes on their own. They don't feed, grow, respire or excrete.
A virus invades a host cell and forces the cell to make copies of the virus.
Think of it like thisA virus is like a pirate USB stick. On its own it does nothing. Plug it into a computer (a cell), and it takes over the computer to make more copies of itself.
Exam tip"Mia thinks a virus is a living organism. Is she correct?" Tick No. Explain: "A virus is not a living organism because it cannot reproduce on its own. It can only replicate inside a living cell, and it cannot carry out all seven life processes."

4.4 What is a species?

A species is a group of organisms that can mate with each other to produce fertile offspring. Fertile means the babies can grow up and have babies of their own.

  • Organisms of the same species usually look similar to one another.
  • They look different from organisms of other species.
  • Every species has a two-part scientific name. The mountain zebra is Equus zebra.
Exam tipWhy are lions and tigers different species, even though they can sometimes reproduce together?
"Organisms belong to the same species only if they reproduce to produce fertile offspring. Lions and tigers produce infertile offspring (ligers), which cannot reproduce." Use the word fertile!

4.5 Classification and dichotomous keys

Classification means putting living things into groups. Scientists do this to make it easier to study and understand the huge variety of life on Earth, a bit like a library sorting books into sections.

A dichotomous key helps you identify an unknown organism. At each step you choose between two options (a pair of features). The prefix "di-" means two, just like in dioxide (two oxygens).

A simple dichotomous key. Every question has exactly two answers.
  • To separate birds from mammals, the best feature is having feathers. Both have eyes and mouths and need water, so those features don't help!
  • An animal with six legs and an exoskeleton (a hard outer skeleton) is an insect.
Exam tipA good key question uses a feature you can see and that splits the group into two clearly. "Is it cute?" is a bad question; "Does it have feathers?" is a good one.

4.6 The zebra question (Q7)

(a) Define species: a group of living things that can reproduce together to produce fertile offspring.
(b) Three other characteristics (besides growth) shown by the zebras: one is eating grass (nutrition), one is walking (movement), one has a foal (reproduction). The worksheet accepts reproduction, respiration, movement. Any three of the seven (except growth) are correct.
(c) Is a virus living? No. It cannot reproduce on its own.

Properties of Materials

Why are pans made of metal but their handles made of plastic? Why is gold used for rings? What makes lemon juice sour?

Big idea: Every material has properties (what it is like and how it behaves). We choose a material for a job because of its properties. Most elements are metals or non-metals, and they have opposite properties.

5.1 Metals vs non-metals

On the Periodic Table (the big chart of all the elements), metals are on the left and in the middle. Non-metals are mostly in the top right corner.

metals   non-metals
MetalsNon-metals
Most are solid at room temperatureMany are gases at room temperature
ShinyDull
Conduct heat well (feel cold to touch)Do not conduct heat well
Conduct electricityDo not conduct electricity
Malleable & ductileBrittle if solid
Sonorous (they ring)Not sonorous
Strong and toughUsually weak
The two famous exceptions Mercury is a metal, but it is the only metal that is liquid at room temperature (it was used in old thermometers).
Carbon in the form of graphite (pencil "lead") is a non-metal that can conduct electricity.
Exam tip"A material is brittle, dull and does not conduct electricity. Metal or non-metal?" → Non-metal. All three are non-metal properties.

Ten metals

Iron, gold, silver, copper, nickel, cobalt, mercury, aluminium, zinc, and the answer key also lists steel.

Good to knowSteel is really an alloy (iron mixed with carbon), not a pure metal. Write what your teacher expects, but if you want to be extra safe, use lead or tin instead.

Five non-metals (other than sulfur & helium)

Carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, chlorine, phosphorus.

Uses of non-metals

  • Sulfur: making sulfuric acid and fertiliser.
  • Helium: balloons, because it is a very light gas and does not react easily, so it is safe (it won't catch fire).

5.4 Alloys and carat gold

An alloy is a mixture of a metal with one or more other elements (often other metals). Mixing makes the metal better: stronger, harder or less likely to rust.

  • Steel = iron + carbon (stronger than iron)
  • Brass = copper + zinc (sonorous, used for bells and trumpets)

Pure gold is very soft, so jewellery gold is mixed with other metals. Gold purity is measured in carats, out of 24.

24-carat gold is nearly pure gold. 18-carat gold is 18 parts out of 24, which is 75% gold.
How to work it out18 ÷ 24 = 0.75 = 75%. (And 12-carat gold would be 12 ÷ 24 = 50%.)

5.5 Changes to metals: rusting and expanding

Rusting (corrosion)

When iron is left in air (oxygen) and moisture (water) over time, it reacts and forms orange-brown rust. This is a chemical reaction called rusting, a type of corrosion.

iron + oxygen + water → rust

Expansion

When most solid metals are heated, they expand (get slightly bigger). The particles vibrate more and push each other further apart.

Watch out!The particles do not get bigger; they just take up more space.

5.7 Boiling and condensing

Boiling: the water in the flask absorbs heat energy and boils into steam (a gas) at 100 °C.

Condensing: the steam travels into the condenser. Cold water flowing around the outside cools the steam, so it condenses back into liquid water and drips into the beaker.

Real lifeBreathe on a cold mirror: your warm breath condenses into tiny water droplets.

5.8 Acids, alkalis and the pH scale

The pH scale goes from 1 to 14 and tells us how acidic or alkaline a liquid is.

  • pH 1–6 = acid. The lower the number, the stronger the acid. pH 1 is strongly acidic.
  • pH 7 = neutral. Pure water is neutral because its pH is 7.
  • pH 8–14 = alkali. The higher the number, the stronger the alkali.

Indicators

Universal indicator changes to a different colour for each pH: red for strong acids, green for neutral, purple for strong alkalis.

Litmus paper: blue litmus turns red in an acid. Red litmus turns blue in an alkali.

Memory trickAcid turns litmus red. Think "Acid = Apple red".

Chen's pH chart answers

  • pH of vinegar = 4
  • Paint stripper (pH 14) turns universal indicator purple
  • pH of a neutral solution = 7
  • Most acidic = lemon juice (pH 2, the lowest number)

5.9 Hazard symbols and lab safety

  • Corrosive means a substance that can damage skin tissues and materials, causing chemical burns. Strong acids and alkalis are often corrosive.
  • Flammable means it catches fire easily.
  • Hazardous to the environment means it can harm plants, fish and nature.
Exam tipWhat should you do if you spill acid? → Wash the area with lots of water (and tell your teacher).
Drawing the labelsAll hazard symbols are a red diamond (a square on its point) with a black picture inside. Corrosive = liquid dripping from two test tubes onto a surface and a hand. Flammable = a flame.

9

Electricity

What is actually moving inside a wire? Why do lamps get dim? And why are plugs made of plastic AND metal?

Big idea: An electric current is a flow of tiny particles called electrons. Current only flows when there is a complete loop (a circuit) from the cell and back again.

9.1 What is electric current?

Electric current is the flow of electrons around a circuit. Electrons are tiny particles found in atoms. In metals, some electrons are free to move, which is why metals conduct electricity.

  • The greater the current, the faster the flow of electrons.
  • Current is measured in amperes (amps), with the unit A.
  • Current is measured with an ammeter.
Think of it like thisA circuit is like a bicycle chain. The cell is the pedals, pushing the chain. The electrons are the links. When you pedal, every link moves at once, all the way round. Break the chain (open a switch) and everything stops.
Watch out!It's electrons that move, not atoms, protons or neutrons.

9.2 Components and circuit symbols

Scientists draw circuits using symbols instead of pictures. Learn these!

SymbolNameIts job in a circuit
CellProvides the energy to make current flow. Long line = positive (+). Two or more cells = a battery.
LampGives out light.
Switch (open)Opens or closes the circuit, to stop or allow the current. Open = OFF.
Switch (closed)The gap is closed, so current flows. Closed = ON.
AmmeterMeasures the current in amps (A).
BuzzerTurns electrical energy into sound energy (makes a buzzing sound).
Memory trickAmmeter measures Amps. Both start with A, and the symbol has an A inside it!

9.3 Measuring current

  • The ammeter must be connected in series, meaning in the same loop as everything else, so all the current flows through it.
  • A multimeter can measure different things. When Sofia switched hers to measure current, she read the amps scale.
Reading a meterFind the scale for the thing you're measuring (here, the one marked A). See which two numbers the needle is between, then count the small marks. Sofia's needle was just past 2, so the reading is 2.2 A.
The needle is just past 2 → 2.2 A

9.4 Series circuits

A series circuit has only one loop. Everything is connected one after another, like beads on a necklace.

If you…What happens
Open the switchThe current stops completely. The loop is broken.
Add another cellMore push, so more current. Lamps shine brighter.
Add more lamps (same cells)Harder for current to flow, so less current. All lamps get dimmer.
One lamp breaksThe loop is broken, so all the other lamps go out.
Measure current at different placesThe current is the same everywhere in a series circuit.
Exam tipCircuit with two cells, a switch, three lamps and an ammeter:
To increase the current: (1) add more cells, (2) remove a lamp.
To decrease the current: (1) remove a cell, (2) add more lamps.
Watch out!Current is not "used up" as it goes round. It stays the same all the way around a series circuit. The energy is used, not the current.

Which circuit lights the lamp AND sounds the buzzer?

You need three things: a cell (energy), a lamp and a buzzer, all in one complete loop. Only circuit B has all three.

9.5 Conductors and insulators

A conductor lets electricity flow through it easily. An insulator does not.

Conductors ⚡Insulators 🛑
Copper, steel, aluminium, iron, silver, any metal, graphitePlastic, wood, rubber, glass, cotton, air
In terms of particlesConductors have free electrons that can move. Insulators do not have free electrons that can move easily.
A plug uses BOTH kinds of material.
  • Why are plug bodies made of plastic? Plastic is an insulator, so it helps prevent electric shocks.
  • Why are plug pins made of metal? Metal is a conductor, so electricity can flow into the appliance.
  • Why are wires coated in plastic? Plastic is a good insulator that prevents electric shocks.
  • Which could NOT make electric wires? Cotton (it's an insulator). Steel, copper and aluminium all conduct.

9.6 Lily's investigation

Lily's battery had two hazard symbols: A = Corrosive (it has chemicals that can burn skin) and B = Hazardous to the environment (dead tree and fish, so don't throw batteries in the bin!). The meter she used to measure current is an ammeter.
